Can a small robot help us spot conditions that favor blue-green algae?

Nature Nexus is exploring how water temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, nutrients and water movement change together — and how an affordable sensor robot could make those changes easier to see.

THE QUESTION

From a lake problem to a testable engineering challenge

Austin reports that conditions favorable to harmful blue-green algae include warm water, low flow and high nutrient levels. The city also notes that field samples are snapshots: conditions can change quickly and vary around the lake.

That gap inspired our challenge: build a system that measures several environmental clues repeatedly and puts them together in one simple dashboard.

Dissolved oxygenOxygen can rise during photosynthesis and fall when biomass respires or decays.
TurbidityTracks how cloudy the water is; useful as context, but not an algae detector by itself.
Phosphate & nitrateNutrients can support excess growth; the team can use a water-intake sample for low-cost testing.
Water movementHelps distinguish still from moving water, an important condition in the team’s hypothesis.

PROPOSED ENGINEERING DESIGN

Floating Water Quality Monitoring Device

Nature Nexus concept

This concept brings the team’s sensor ideas into one floating platform. The device is designed to collect water-quality readings, take small water samples for nitrate and phosphate testing, log results, and send measurements to a phone or computer for later comparison.

01

Continuous sensors

Temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and turbidity can be measured repeatedly so the team can study how conditions change over time.

02

Nitrate & phosphate sampling

A small water sample can be pumped into a mixing chamber, combined with a reagent and measured with a color sensor. This is a proposed prototype workflow that the team can simplify and validate in controlled tub testing.

03

Data logging & communication

An SD-card module can save readings while Wi-Fi or another wireless module can send results to a laptop or dashboard for graphing and comparison.

04

Built for experiments first

The team can begin with a stationary floating prototype in a tub, prove each sensor and sampling step, then add mobility, mapping, phycocyanin sensing and AI only after the basic measurements are reliable.

Important scientific limit

This device is intended to monitor environmental conditions and collect screening measurements. It does not by itself prove that cyanobacteria or toxins are present, and it should not be used to declare water safe or unsafe.

RESEARCH NOTEBOOK

Build → test → learn → improve

STEP 1

Understand the problem

Study Lady Bird Lake, cyanobacteria, toxins, nutrients, flow and existing monitoring limitations.

STEP 2

Talk with experts

Use scientist and watershed conversations to discover what data matter, what is difficult to measure and where a student prototype could help.

STEP 3

Build one sensor at a time

Verify each connection and reading before adding another sensor. Current progress: water temperature verified; turbidity added to the build.

STEP 4

Run controlled tub tests

Change one condition at a time, record data and compare repeat trials. Avoid culturing or handling potentially toxic cyanobacteria.

STEP 5

Integrate the robot

Add mobility or a sampling mechanism so the system can gather readings from different locations in a test tub.

STEP 6

Explain uncertainty

Show what the sensors can tell us — and what they cannot. Confirming cyanobacteria or toxins requires appropriate biological or laboratory testing.

Research · Sensors · Remote Sensing · AI

Mr. Chintan Maniyar

PhD candidate, University of Georgia · cyanobacterial bloom research

Meeting notes verified from recording

What we discussed

  • How researchers process lake-water samples and build lower-cost sensors for bloom monitoring.
  • Why satellite images are useful for finding possible bloom locations, but must be checked against measurements collected on the water.
  • How light-based measurements and a spectrometer can be used to estimate cyanobacterial abundance.
  • Why warm temperatures and high nutrient levels are major drivers of blooms, and why runoff can carry nutrients into lakes.
  • Why blooms are often more severe in slow or stagnant water than in fast-moving water.
  • How AI can learn from spectral/color data to estimate whether cyanobacteria may be present and how much.
  • The difference between chlorophyll and phycocyanin: chlorophyll occurs in many algae, while phycocyanin is much more useful for identifying cyanobacteria.
  • A safer controlled experiment using a large container, non-toxic materials such as spirulina/chlorophyll, added nutrients and controlled temperature.

What the kids learned

  • One sensor is not enough. A stronger system combines several water-quality clues.
  • Ground truth matters. Satellite or AI results should be checked using real measurements from the water.
  • Phycocyanin is important. It could make the future robot more specific to cyanobacteria than chlorophyll alone.
  • AI needs data. The team first needs repeatable sensor readings before building a useful prediction model.
  • Controlled testing is safer and clearer. Changing one factor at a time helps show what caused a result.
How this changed our project

The team moved toward a multi-sensor Water Scout: temperature, pH, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, nutrients and water movement now form the core environmental measurements, with phycocyanin and AI as future upgrades.

Austin Watershed · Ecology · Field Research

Dr. Brent Bellinger

Austin Watershed Protection · blue-green algae and reservoir research

Expert feedback incorporated

What we discussed

  • The team presented its Lady Bird Lake research and asked where important unanswered questions still exist.
  • Dr. Bellinger pointed the team toward research needs and challenges described in current scientific work.
  • He highlighted the role of fine sediment becoming trapped or moved within cyanobacterial mats as an especially interesting question.
  • He also encouraged the team to think about the microchemistry inside the mats and what could realistically be tested on a small scale.

What the kids learned

  • The visible mat is only part of the story. Conditions inside and underneath a mat can be very different from the surrounding water.
  • Sediment can matter. The team should consider what is happening at the lake bottom, not only at the surface.
  • A good innovation starts with a gap. Instead of repeating existing solutions, the kids should identify something that is difficult to measure or monitor today.
How this changed our project

The discussion pushed Nature Nexus to treat the robot as a research and monitoring tool rather than simply an algae-removal machine, and to think about sampling different locations and conditions instead of relying on one surface reading.

Engineering · Monitoring · Testing · Real-World Feasibility

Dr. Vijay Chopakattla & Mr. Tom

Expert discussion with Nature Nexus · October 14, 2026

Meeting recording received

What we discussed

  • How the team could turn its blue-green algae research into a practical engineering system rather than relying on a single test or observation.
  • Why the first goal should be reliable monitoring and repeatable measurements before trying to automate a larger treatment or response system.
  • How several environmental measurements can be collected together so the team can compare conditions and look for patterns over time.
  • The importance of testing the prototype in a controlled setup, where the kids can change one condition at a time and compare readings before and after a change.
  • Why an engineering solution has to be practical: sensors must be calibrated, protected from water, positioned consistently and tested for repeatability.
  • How a future system could use the collected data to identify areas that deserve closer investigation instead of claiming that one reading proves a toxic bloom.

What the kids learned

  • Detection and treatment are different problems. First understand what is happening in the water; then decide what action is appropriate.
  • Good data must be repeatable. A reading is more useful when the team can reproduce it under the same conditions.
  • Build in stages. Prove each sensor and experiment separately before combining everything into one robot.
  • Engineering means testing assumptions. The team should compare its predictions with actual measurements instead of assuming a condition automatically means algae is present.
  • Real-world use changes the design. Waterproofing, sampling position, calibration, power and easy maintenance matter just as much as the code.
How this changed our project

The discussion reinforced Nature Nexus's decision to build a modular Water Scout. Instead of trying to solve every part of the algae problem at once, the kids are validating temperature and turbidity first, then adding pH, dissolved oxygen, nutrients and water movement. Their next goal is to run controlled tub experiments, compare repeat readings, and only then add mobility, mapping and AI pattern recognition.
